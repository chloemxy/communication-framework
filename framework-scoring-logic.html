① Clarity & Content Quality
CCI
CDC Clear Communication Index
Clarity ✅ Scored
How it works
20 binary (Yes/No) items across 5 domains. Each "Yes" = 1 point. Final score is a percentage of Yes answers. A score ≥90% (18+ items passing) is required to pass.
Scoring formula
Score = (# of Yes items / 20) × 100
≥90%Pass (18–20 items)
<90%Fail — revise
The 5 domains & items
Domain# ItemsWhat it checks
Main Message7Is the main message stated, actionable, specific, and at the front? Is a call to action present?
Language4Active voice, everyday words, personal pronouns (you/your), short sentences (≤20 words avg)
Information3Behavioral recommendations have a reason; numbers presented in context; only essential info included
Numbers3Numbers meaningful to audience; fractions/percentages explained; numbers not overused
Risk3Both sides of risk shown; absolute vs. relative risk explained; baseline risk provided
Items 1–7 (main message) carry the most weight in practice — if those fail, the piece rarely passes. CCI does not distinguish severity of failure; every item counts equally.
Sources

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. (2019). CDC Clear Communication Index: A Tool for Developing and Assessing CDC Public Communication Products. U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. cdc.gov/ccindex

Baur C, Prue C. (2014). The CDC Clear Communication Index is a new evidence-based tool to prepare and review health information. Health Promotion Practice, 15(5), 629–637. doi:10.1177/1524839914538969

PEMAT
Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (AHRQ)
Clarity ✅ Scored
How it works
PEMAT produces two separate scores — Understandability and Actionability — never combined. Two versions exist: PEMAT-P (print) and PEMAT-A/V (audiovisual). Items rated Agree (1), Disagree (0), or N/A (excluded from denominator).
Scoring formula
Understandability % = (Σ agrees on U items / # applicable U items) × 100
Actionability % = (Σ agrees on A items / # applicable A items) × 100
>70%Both scores must exceed this
Understandability items (PEMAT-P — 17 items)
Category# ItemsChecks
Content4Main message clear; not overloaded; accurate; required reading level (≤8th grade)
Word Choice & Language2Common everyday words; acronyms defined
Use of Numbers4Numbers communicated clearly; fractions/percentages explained; visual representations used
Organization2Logical flow; summary or conclusion present
Layout & Design3Visual supports comprehension; tables clear; no distracting clutter
Use of Visuals2Images/charts reinforce rather than conflict with text
Actionability items (PEMAT-P — 7 items)
  • Explicit statement of what the reader should do
  • Action steps are doable (not vague)
  • Tools or aids provided to help take action (e.g., tracking sheet, checklist)
  • Patient can identify next step from the material
  • Contact information or referral path provided
  • Visual representation of steps (where applicable)
  • Timing for action specified
Sources

Shoemaker SJ, Wolf MS, Brach C. (2014). Development of the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT): A new measure of understandability and actionability for print and audiovisual patient information. Patient Education and Counseling, 96(3), 395–403. doi:10.1016/j.pec.2014.05.027

Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. PEMAT and User's Guide. ahrq.gov/health-literacy/patient-education/pemat

SAM
Suitability Assessment of Materials
Clarity ✅ Scored
How it works
22 factors across 6 categories. Each factor rated on a 3-point scale: 2 (Superior), 1 (Adequate), 0 (Not Suitable). Total points divided by maximum possible, expressed as a percentage. N/A items are excluded from the denominator.
Scoring formula
Score % = (Σ points scored / Σ max possible points) × 100
70–100%Superior
40–69%Adequate
<40%Not Suitable
The 6 categories & 22 factors
Category# FactorsWhat it evaluates
Content6Purpose stated; content matches literacy level; scope limited; summary included; reading level (Fry); common-use vocabulary
Literacy Demand7Reading grade level (≤5th preferred); writing style (active/conversational); vocabulary (common words); context established; learning aids; sentence length
Graphics4Cover graphic draws interest; type of visuals appropriate; captions used; visuals are culturally relevant and unambiguous
Layout & Typography3Layout factors (columns, white space); typography (font size ≥12pt); subheadings break up text
Learning Stimulation & Motivation2Interaction (questions/problems); behavior modeling (positive)
Cultural Appropriateness2Images/examples match target culture; cultural logic respected
SAM is the most granular of the content auditing tools. A "0" on any Content factor should be treated as a blocker regardless of total score.
Sources

Doak CC, Doak LG, Root JH. (1996). Teaching Patients with Low Literacy Skills (2nd ed.). J.B. Lippincott. (SAM instrument: pp. 41–59)

Winslow EH. (2001). Patient education materials: Can patients read them, or are they ending up in the trash? American Journal of Nursing, 101(10), 33–38. doi:10.1097/00000446-200110000-00020 (validation reference)

DISCERN
DISCERN Quality Instrument for Consumer Health Information
Clarity ✅ Scored
How it works
16 questions across 3 sections. Questions 1–15 rated 1–5 (1 = No/Poor, 5 = Yes/Excellent). Question 16 is an overall quality rating (1–5), kept separate. No universal pass/fail — scores are relative and comparative.
Scoring
Part 1 score: sum of Q1–8 (range 8–40)
Part 2 score: sum of Q9–15 (range 7–35)
Q16: standalone overall rating (1–5)
No absolute threshold — use comparatively. Published benchmarks: peer-reviewed health leaflets average ~3.5–4/5 per question.
Part 1: Reliability (Q1–8)
  • Q1: Clear aims stated at the beginning?
  • Q2: Does it achieve its aims?
  • Q3: Relevant to patients?
  • Q4: Explicit sources of information?
  • Q5: Date of information stated?
  • Q6: Balanced and unbiased?
  • Q7: Provides details of additional sources?
  • Q8: Refers to areas of uncertainty?
Part 2: Treatment Choices (Q9–15)
  • Q9: Describes how the treatment works?
  • Q10: Describes benefits of each treatment?
  • Q11: Describes risks of each treatment?
  • Q12: Describes what would happen without treatment?
  • Q13: Describes how choices may affect quality of life?
  • Q14: Is it clear there may be more than one treatment?
  • Q15: Provides support for shared decision-making?
Sources

Charnock D, Shepperd S, Needham G, Gann R. (1999). DISCERN: An instrument for judging the quality of written consumer health information on treatment choices. Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health, 53(2), 105–111. doi:10.1136/jech.53.2.105

DISCERN Online. University of Oxford & British Library. discern.org.uk

EQIP
Ensuring Quality Information for Patients
Clarity ✅ Scored
How it works
36 items across 8 sections. Each item rated Yes (1), No (0), or N/A (excluded). Final score = percentage of applicable items rated Yes. No universal pass threshold; the tool is normative/comparative across document versions.
Scoring formula
Score % = (# Yes items / # applicable items) × 100
Separate scores can be computed per section for diagnostic use.
The 8 sections
SectionFocus
1. IdentityAuthorship, source organization, date, version clearly stated
2. PurposeDocument purpose and target audience explicitly stated
3. ContentAccurate, relevant, complete information on condition and treatment options
4. RecommendationsSpecific, actionable recommendations provided; basis for recommendations explained
5. ComprehensibilityLanguage appropriate; jargon avoided; short sentences; active voice
6. Layout & DesignLogical structure; headings used; white space; font size adequate
7. IllustrationsVisuals present, appropriate, and labeled
8. References / SourcesEvidence cited; references listed; evidence base dated
Sources

Moult B, Franck LS, Brady H. (2004). Ensuring quality information for patients: Development and preliminary validation of a new instrument to improve the quality of written health care information. Health Expectations, 7(2), 165–175. doi:10.1111/j.1369-7625.2004.00273.x

Readability
Flesch-Kincaid / SMOG / Flesch Reading Ease
Clarity ✅ Scored
Flesch Reading Ease
FRE = 206.835 − (1.015 × ASL) − (84.6 × ASW)
ASL = Average Sentence Length (words/sentence) · ASW = Average Syllables per Word
60–70Standard / Plain
50–59Fairly Difficult
<50Difficult
Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level
FKGL = (0.39 × ASL) + (11.8 × ASW) − 15.59
6–8Target for health materials
9–10Borderline
>10Too complex
SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook)
SMOG Grade = √(Polysyllabic word count × [30 / # sentences]) + 3
Count polysyllabic words (3+ syllables) in 30 sentences (10 from start, 10 from middle, 10 from end). Most accurate when ≥30 sentences available.
≤8Suitable (6th–8th grade)
9–10Review
>10Simplify
SMOG is considered the most accurate for health materials because it accounts for the density of medical polysyllabic terms. Use it as your primary readability tool; FRE and FKGL as secondary checks.
Sources

Flesch R. (1948). A new readability yardstick. Journal of Applied Psychology, 32(3), 221–233. doi:10.1037/h0057532

Kincaid JP, Fishburne RP Jr, Rogers RL, Chissom BS. (1975). Derivation of New Readability Formulas (Automated Readability Index, Fog Count and Flesch Reading Ease Formula) for Navy Enlisted Personnel. Research Branch Report 8-75. Naval Technical Training Command. DTIC ADA006655

McLaughlin GH. (1969). SMOG grading: A new readability formula. Journal of Reading, 12(8), 639–646. (International Reading Association)

Paasche-Orlow MK, Taylor HA, Brancati FL. (2003). Readability standards for informed-consent forms as compared with actual readability. New England Journal of Medicine, 348(8), 721–726. doi:10.1056/NEJMsa021212 (validates Grade 6–8 target)

Plain Language
U.S. Federal Plain Language Guidelines (PlainLanguage.gov)
Clarity ❌ No score
How it works
A prescriptive editing checklist, not a scoring rubric. Organized into 5 principles — applied during drafting and revision, not post-hoc auditing.
The 5 principles & key rules
PrincipleKey rules
1. Write for your audienceIdentify who will read it; use "you" and "we"; address their questions, not your org's
2. Organize for the readerPut most important info first; use headers; use Q&A format where possible
3. Choose words carefullyCommon, everyday words; define jargon if unavoidable; avoid legalese/bureaucratese; no nominalizations (use "apply" not "make an application")
4. Use short sentences & paragraphs≤20 words per sentence (target); one idea per paragraph; use active voice; positive framing
5. Use design to aid comprehensionBullet lists for series of 3+; tables for complex data; white space; headings parallel in structure
Sources

Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN). (2011). Federal Plain Language Guidelines. PlainLanguage.gov. plainlanguage.gov/guidelines

Plain Writing Act of 2010, Pub. L. 111-274, 124 Stat. 2861 (2010). govinfo.gov

NCI Pink Book
National Cancer Institute — Making Health Communication Programs Work
Clarity ❌ No score
How it works
A planning and process guide for health communication campaigns. Not a rubric — it structures the process of creating and testing materials. Success is demonstrated through pre/post audience testing, not by a checklist score.
The 6-phase planning process
PhaseWhat happens
1. Planning & StrategyDefine problem, audience, objective, channels, resources
2. Developing ConceptsFormative research; concept testing with target audience; message mapping
3. Developing MaterialsDraft materials; pretesting; iterative revision
4. ImplementationChannel selection; dissemination planning; partnerships
5. Assessing EffectivenessProcess and outcome evaluation; reach and awareness measurement
6. Feedback to RefineUse findings to improve future communication
The Pink Book mandates audience testing at Phase 2 and 3. A material that hasn't been tested with its target audience fails the Pink Book standard — regardless of how it scores on CCI or SAM.
Sources

National Cancer Institute. (2004). Making Health Communication Programs Work ("The Pink Book"). U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, NIH Publication No. 04-5145. cancer.gov/publications/health-communication/pink-book.pdf

② Process & Interaction Quality
OPTION Scale
Observing Patient Involvement in Decision Making
Process ✅ Scored
How it works
A trained observer (not the patient) rates a clinical consultation — live or recorded. 12 items, each scored 0–4. Two versions exist: OPTION-5 (condensed) and OPTION-12 (full). The score reflects the clinician's skill at involving the patient, not patient satisfaction.
Scoring formula
Raw total = Σ of 12 items (0–48 range)
Rescaled: (Raw total / 48) × 100 → 0–100
0 = behavior not observed; 1 = minimal attempt; 2 = notable effort; 3 = consistent behavior; 4 = fully applied, exemplary
The 12 items
  • Clinician draws attention to there being a problem that requires a decision
  • States that there is more than one way to deal with the problem
  • Assesses the preferred format for information delivery
  • Lists options including the option of no active treatment
  • Explains pros and cons of each option
  • Explores the patient's expectations or ideas about how the problem is to be managed
  • Explores the patient's concerns about each option
  • Checks that the patient has understood the information
  • Explores whether the patient has any preferred option
  • Negotiates a decision with the patient
  • Indicates a need to review the decision
  • Indicates that the doctor has deferred to the patient's preferred option
No single benchmark threshold exists — OPTION is used for research comparisons and training feedback, not pass/fail. Published studies report means of 20–40/100 in real-world primary care consultations.
Sources

Elwyn G, Edwards A, Wensing M, Hood K, Atwell C, Grol R. (2003). Shared decision making: Developing the OPTION scale for measuring patient involvement. Quality and Safety in Health Care, 12(2), 93–99. doi:10.1136/qhc.12.2.93

Elwyn G, Hutchings H, Edwards A, et al. (2005). The OPTION scale: Measuring the extent that clinicians involve patients in decision-making tasks. Health Expectations, 8(1), 34–42. doi:10.1111/j.1369-7625.2004.00311.x (reliability/validity)

IPDAS
International Patient Decision Aid Standards
Process ⚠️ Semi-scored
How it works
Two-tier criteria: Qualifying criteria (must pass all to call it a decision aid) and Certifying criteria (for formal certification). Items are Yes/No with some level-of-evidence ratings.
Qualifying criteria (6 — must meet ALL)
  • Describes the health condition for which the index decision is required
  • Explicitly states the index decision
  • Describes the options available for the index decision
  • Describes the positive features (benefits or advantages) of each option
  • Describes the negative features (harms, side effects, disadvantages) of each option
  • Describes what it is like to experience the consequences of the options
Certifying criteria (grouped domains)
DomainKey items
InformationProbabilities presented; absolute risk used; time horizon explicit; uncertainty acknowledged
ProbabilitiesEvent rates in same denominator; comparison group stated; sources cited; date of evidence
Values ClarificationFeatures most important to patients described; exercise to clarify values included
Guidance / CoachingSteps to decision-making described; preparation for consultation included; no pressure toward one option
DevelopmentSystematic development process; patient/clinician involvement; plain language review; field testing done
EvidenceSystematic review of evidence; update policy stated
DisclosureFunding source; conflict of interest disclosed
Plain LanguageReading level ≤8th grade; layout aids comprehension

Elwyn G, O'Connor A, Stacey D, et al.; International Patient Decision Aids Standards (IPDAS) Collaboration. (2006). Developing a quality criteria framework for patient decision aids: Online international Delphi consensus process. BMJ, 333(7565), 417. doi:10.1136/bmj.38926.629329.AE

Stacey D, Légaré F, Pouliot S, Kryworuchko J, Dunn S. (2010). Shared decision making models to inform an interprofessional perspective on decision support for people facing health decisions. Patient Education and Counseling, 80(2), 164–172. doi:10.1016/j.pec.2009.10.015

Ottawa DMSF
Ottawa Decision Support Framework + Decisional Conflict Scale
Process ❌ Framework (DCS is scored)
How it works
The DMSF itself is a design and counseling framework (not scored). It's always paired with the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) to measure whether an intervention reduced conflict. The DCS is the measurement instrument.
Ottawa DMSF — 3 components
ComponentWhat it includes
Decision needs assessmentIdentify: patient's knowledge gaps, unclear values, support needs, communication barriers, prior decision-making experience
Decision support interventionsProvide: risk communication, values clarification exercises, social support, deliberation aids
Decisions, outcomes, qualityEvaluate: decision quality (informed + values-concordant), decision process quality, health outcomes
Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) — the scoring companion
16 items (or 10-item low-literacy version), 5-point Likert (0 = strongly agree, 4 = strongly disagree). Items reversed and summed.
DCS Score = (sum / 64) × 25 → range 0–100
<25Low conflict — decision supported
25–37.4Moderate conflict
≥37.5High conflict — action needed
DCS subscales
  • Informed: knows enough about options, risks, benefits
  • Values clarity: knows which benefits and risks matter most to them
  • Support: has enough support and advice
  • Uncertainty: uncertain about the choice
  • Effective decision: feels decision is informed, value-based, supported
Sources

O'Connor AM. (1995). Validation of a decisional conflict scale. Medical Decision Making, 15(1), 25–30. doi:10.1177/0272989X9501500105

O'Connor AM, Tugwell P, Wells GA, et al. (1998). A decision aid for women considering hormone therapy after menopause: Decision support framework and evaluation. Patient Education and Counseling, 33(3), 267–279. doi:10.1016/S0738-3991(98)00026-3 (DMSF framework paper)

Ottawa Hospital Research Institute. Decision Support & Decision Aids. decisionaid.ohri.ca

Teach-Back / Ask Me 3
Teach-Back Method & Ask Me 3 Patient Questions
Process ❌ No score
Teach-Back — how it works
Clinician explains information, then asks the patient to explain it back in their own words. If the patient cannot, the clinician re-explains (not blamed on the patient — "I must not have explained that well"). Repeated until patient can accurately demonstrate understanding. Pass/fail qualitative.
Teach-Back assessment criteria
ResultWhat it means
PassPatient explains the key information accurately in their own words, demonstrates the skill, or can describe the next step without prompting
PartialPatient understands main idea but misses critical details — re-teach specific gaps
FailSignificant misunderstanding — full re-explanation needed
Ask Me 3 — the 3 questions patients should be able to answer
  • Q1: What is my main problem?
  • Q2: What do I need to do?
  • Q3: Why is it important for me to do this?
For material design: if a patient can't answer all 3 Ask Me 3 questions after reading/viewing your material alone, the material needs revision — regardless of CCI or readability scores.
Sources

Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. (2020). Use the Teach-Back Method: Tool #5. Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit. ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5

Schillinger D, Piette J, Grumbach K, et al. (2003). Closing the loop: Physician communication with diabetic patients who have low health literacy. Archives of Internal Medicine, 163(1), 83–90. doi:10.1001/archinte.163.1.83 (foundational teach-back outcomes study)

Pfizer. (n.d.). Ask Me 3: Good Questions for Your Good Health. National Patient Safety Foundation. npsf.org/page/askme3

HBM
Health Belief Model
Behavior ❌ No score
How it works
A message design framework based on 6 cognitive constructs. Each construct is a lever — content must address all relevant ones to motivate behavior. Constructs are measured via validated Likert-scale surveys for research; in design practice, they're used as a checklist.
The 6 constructs
ConstructDefinitionMessage strategy
Perceived SusceptibilityBelief that one can get the condition"People like you are at risk…"
Perceived SeverityBelief about the seriousness of the condition/consequences"Untreated, this leads to…"
Perceived BenefitsBelief that the recommended action will reduce risk or harm"Taking this medication reduces your risk by X%"
Perceived BarriersBelief about the costs or obstacles of taking actionAddress objections directly; simplify access
Cues to ActionExternal or internal triggers that prompt behaviorReminder cards; symptom awareness campaigns
Self-EfficacyConfidence in ability to perform the action"Here's exactly how to do it in 3 steps…"
The most common HBM design error: content addresses Susceptibility and Severity but neglects Barriers and Self-Efficacy — resulting in fear without action.
Sources

Rosenstock IM. (1974). Historical origins of the health belief model. Health Education Monographs, 2(4), 328–335. doi:10.1177/109019817400200403

Janz NK, Becker MH. (1984). The health belief model: A decade later. Health Education Quarterly, 11(1), 1–47. doi:10.1177/109019818401100101

Champion VL, Skinner CS. (2008). The health belief model. In K Glanz, BK Rimer, K Viswanath (Eds.), Health Behavior and Health Education: Theory, Research, and Practice (4th ed., pp. 45–65). Jossey-Bass.

TTM
Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)
Behavior ❌ No score
How it works
Classifies individuals into 1 of 5 stages based on their readiness to change a specific behavior. Different interventions are effective at different stages. Stage is assessed via validated staging algorithms or questionnaires.
The 5 stages
StageDefinitionWhat they needAppropriate intervention
Pre-contemplationNot thinking about changing (within 6 months)Awareness of risk; consciousness-raisingEducation about consequences; normalization
ContemplationAware of the problem, considering change (within 6 months)Help weighing pros and consMotivational content; testimonials; decisional balance
PreparationIntending to act within 30 days; may be making small changesSpecific plan and resourcesAction planning tools; How-to guides
ActionHas made the change (within past 6 months)Support and reinforcementProgress tracking; problem-solving aids
MaintenanceSustained change for >6 monthsRelapse preventionLong-term habit support; coping strategies
Key TTM companion constructs
  • Decisional Balance: pros vs. cons of changing; shifts across stages
  • Self-Efficacy: confidence to maintain change in tempting situations
  • Processes of Change: 10 cognitive/behavioral strategies that drive stage movement
Sources

Prochaska JO, DiClemente CC. (1983). Stages and processes of self-change of smoking: Toward an integrative model of change. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 51(3), 390–395. doi:10.1037/0022-006X.51.3.390

Prochaska JO, Velicer WF. (1997). The transtheoretical model of health behavior change. American Journal of Health Promotion, 12(1), 38–48. doi:10.4278/0890-1171-12.1.38

EPPM
Extended Parallel Process Model
Behavior ❌ No score
How it works
A framework for designing fear-appeal messages (risk communication). Predicts whether high-threat messages lead to behavior change (danger control) or backfire (fear control / boomerang). The key ratio is Perceived Threat vs. Perceived Efficacy.
The 4 constructs (measured via 4-point Likert)
ConstructWhat it measures
Perceived Susceptibility"I am at risk for this"
Perceived Severity"This condition is serious"
Response Efficacy"This recommended action actually works"
Self-Efficacy"I can perform this recommended action"
The 2 pathways
ConditionResult
High threat + High efficacyDanger control → behavior change (what you want)
High threat + Low efficacyFear control → denial, avoidance, anger, boomerang (what you don't want)
Low threatNo motivation to change — message ignored
Design rule: never raise perceived threat without simultaneously and convincingly raising efficacy. Every fear appeal must pair "here's the risk" with "here's what works and here's how you can do it."
Sources

Witte K. (1992). Putting the fear back into fear appeals: The Extended Parallel Process Model. Communication Monographs, 59(4), 329–349. doi:10.1080/03637759209376276

Witte K, Allen M. (2000). A meta-analysis of fear appeals: Implications for effective public health campaigns. Health Education & Behavior, 27(5), 591–615. doi:10.1177/109019810002700506

WHO Risk Comms
WHO Risk Communication Framework
Behavior ❌ No score
How it works
A set of principles and operational standards for public health communication during emergencies or health crises. No numerical scoring — evaluated through process indicators (did you do this?) and outcome indicators (trust, behavior change).
Core principles
PrincipleWhat it requires
TrustBuild credibility before the crisis; disclose conflicts; use trusted community messengers
Early announcementCommunicate early even with incomplete information; silence creates rumor
TransparencyShare what is known and unknown; acknowledge uncertainty; correct errors publicly
Respect publicListen to community concerns; respond to rumors; include affected communities in planning
PlanningPre-establish channels, roles, and messages; coordinate across agencies; train spokespeople
RCCE (Risk Communication & Community Engagement) — the applied form
WHO operationalizes its framework through RCCE plans that specify: audiences, channels, message calendar, community feedback loops, rumor monitoring, and evaluation metrics (reach, sentiment, behavior indicators).
Sources

World Health Organization. (2017). Communicating Risk in Public Health Emergencies: A WHO Guideline for Emergency Risk Communication (ERC) Policy and Practice. WHO. who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550208

Covello VT, Peters RG, Wojtecki JG, Hyde RC. (2001). Risk communication, the West Nile Virus epidemic, and bioterrorism: Responding to the communication challenges posed by the intentional or unintentional release of a pathogen in an urban setting. Journal of Urban Health, 78(2), 382–391. doi:10.1093/jurban/78.2.382

④ Outcomes & Experience
PAM
Patient Activation Measure (Hibbard et al.)
Outcomes ✅ Scored
How it works
13 items (PAM-13) or 22 items (PAM-22). Scored via Item Response Theory (IRT) — not a simple sum. Each item answered on a 4-point scale (Disagree Strongly → Agree Strongly, plus N/A). Raw responses are converted to a 0–100 score using Insignia's licensed scoring algorithm.
The 4 activation levels
≤47.0Level 1 — Disengaged/Overwhelmed
47.1–55.1Level 2 — Aware but struggling
55.2–67.0Level 3 — Taking action
≥67.1Level 4 — Maintaining behaviors
What PAM-13 measures (sample items)
DomainSample item
Role understanding"When it comes to managing my health, I am the one responsible"
Confidence"I am confident I can tell when I need to seek medical care"
Knowledge & skills"I know what each of my prescribed medications does"
Self-management"I can maintain lifestyle changes in difficult times"
PAM is proprietary (Insignia Health). Commercial licensing is required to use the scoring algorithm. A clinically meaningful change is a 3–6 point increase over 6–12 months. Moving one full level is a significant intervention effect.

CAHPS / HCAHPS
Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems
Outcomes ✅ Scored
How it works
HCAHPS (Hospital version) is the federally mandated patient experience survey. 29 items (21 core patient-facing). Responses are reduced to Top-Box scores — the percentage of respondents selecting the most favorable response category. Scores are publicly reported after risk adjustment for patient mix.
Scoring method
Top-Box % = (# "Always" or "9–10" responses / total responses) × 100
Publicly reported scores are case-mix adjusted to account for patient characteristics (age, health status, education, service line).
HCAHPS domains (10 composite measures publicly reported)
DomainItems
Nurse Communication3 items: nurses listen, explain, treat with courtesy
Doctor Communication3 items: doctors listen, explain, treat with courtesy
Staff Responsiveness2 items: call button, bathroom assistance response time
Communication about Medicines2 items: explained new meds, side effects discussed
Discharge Information2 items: written discharge info, symptoms to watch for
Care Transitions3 items: understood care after discharge, questions answered, preferences understood
Cleanliness1 item: room always clean
Quietness1 item: room always quiet at night
Overall Hospital Rating1 item: 0–10 global rating
Willingness to Recommend1 item: Definitely Yes / Probably Yes / No
CAHPS is a family of surveys — different versions exist for clinicians (CG-CAHPS), home health, nursing homes, dialysis, and more. Each version uses the same Top-Box methodology but different items.

NPS
Net Promoter Score
Outcomes ✅ Scored
How it works
A single 0–10 "Recommend" question. Respondents are classified into 3 groups; the score is the gap between Promoters and Detractors.
Scoring formula
Promoters: scored 9–10 · Passives: scored 7–8 · Detractors: scored 0–6
NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors
>70Excellent (World-class)
50–70Very Good
0–49Average to Good
<0Needs improvement
Healthcare sector benchmarks typically run 20–50. Compare within your own sector, not against general consumer benchmarks.
NPS is a directional indicator, not a diagnostic tool. A low NPS tells you there's a problem; it doesn't tell you where. Pair with open-ended "Why?" questions to make it actionable.

Beryl PX

Outcomes ❌ No score
How it works
An organizational framework defining patient experience as "the sum of all interactions, shaped by an organization's culture, that influence patient perceptions across the continuum of care." Used for org-level strategy and culture change, not individual material evaluation.
4 pillars of the framework
PillarFocus
Culture & LeadershipExecutive commitment; values alignment; accountability structures for PX
Infrastructure & GovernanceDedicated PX roles; patient advisory councils; feedback systems
Evidence & MeasurementData collection (CAHPS, NPS); outcome tracking; benchmarking; continuous improvement cycles

Beryl's annual "State of Patient Experience" benchmarking report is a useful calibration tool — it shows how healthcare orgs are prioritizing PX investment and where gaps exist industry-wide.

EBCD
Experience-Based Co-Design
Outcomes ❌ No score
How it works
A participatory design methodology where patients and staff together identify emotional experience touchpoints and co-design improvements. Outputs are qualitative: prioritized improvement themes, prototyped solutions, and implementation commitments. No scoring; success is measured by implemented changes and patient/staff-reported improvement.
The 6-phase process
PhaseWhat happens
1. Set upDefine scope; recruit patient and staff participants; ethical approval
2. Staff experienceObserve care; interview staff about their experience; identify touchpoints
3. Patient experienceInterview patients (filmed where possible); emotional touchpoint mapping
4. Share & bring togetherShare edited patient films with staff; identify shared touchpoints; joint event
5. Co-designMixed patient-staff co-design groups work on priority touchpoints; prototype solutions
6. Celebrate & sharePresent changes; evaluate impact; sustainability planning

SEIPS
Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety (SEIPS 2.0)
Outcomes ❌ No score
How it works
A systems model for analyzing healthcare work systems and their relationship to patient safety outcomes. Used to frame design decisions in clinical contexts. The model is analytical — no score; outputs are work system analyses and redesign recommendations.
SEIPS 2.0 model components
LayerComponents
Work SystemPerson(s) · Tasks · Tools & Technologies · Organization · Environment — all interact together
ProcessesCare processes (clinical) · Collaborative processes (team) · Patient processes (self-care)
OutcomesPatient outcomes (safety, quality of life) · Professional outcomes (burnout, satisfaction) · Organizational outcomes (efficiency, accreditation)
How to use it in design
  • Map the work system for your target process (e.g., medication reconciliation at discharge)
  • Identify misalignments between any two components that create unsafe or poor-experience processes
  • Design interventions that improve the work system (not just the individual behavior)
  • Measure downstream outcome changes as the evaluation
